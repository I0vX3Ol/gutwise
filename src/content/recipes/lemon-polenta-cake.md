There is a particular social cost to this diet that has nothing to do with symptoms. Someone has a birthday, you bring something, and either you explain the whole protocol to a room of people who did not ask, or you bring a cake you cannot eat.

This is the one that solves it, because it is not a low-FODMAP version of anything. Lemon polenta cake is a real Italian cake that happens, entirely by accident, to contain nothing you need to avoid.

## What replaces the flour

Two things, doing different jobs.

Ground almonds provide fat and protein, which is where the moisture and the dense, slightly fudgy crumb come from. Polenta — milled corn, nothing more — provides the structure and the faint golden graininess that makes the texture distinctive rather than merely gluten-free.

Neither is a substitute. This is how the cake has always been made.

## How you cut it is part of the recipe

Almond meal is low FODMAP at 24 g per serving. Above that the GOS content starts to matter.

A 200 g cake cut into twelve slices puts roughly 17 g in each — comfortable headroom. The same cake cut into eight puts 25 g in each, which is over the line before you have eaten anything else that day.

This is worth internalising because it is a pattern that repeats across the whole protocol: the same food is safe or not depending only on how much of it lands on the plate. Nothing about the cake changes. The knife decides.

## The syrup step matters

It is tempting to skip, and the cake is noticeably worse without it.

Almond-and-polenta cakes are drier than wheat cakes by construction — there is no gluten network holding water. The lemon syrup is what puts the moisture back, and it has to go on while the cake is hot and the tin is still on the rack, so it soaks rather than pools.

Prick the surface more thoroughly than feels necessary, and add the syrup in three or four goes rather than all at once.
