The snack and side aisle is where this diet gets most tedious. Crisps are usually seasoned with onion or garlic powder even when the flavour on the packet suggests otherwise. Oven chips are frequently dusted with wheat flour. Anything battered is out, and most dips start with garlic.

Polenta sidesteps all of it, because corn is one of the very few starches with no FODMAP ceiling at all.

## Why the two-stage method

It looks like a lot of steps for chips. It is really one step, a wait, and a second step, and the wait is unattended.

Wet polenta is a porridge. To get a chip you have to set it into a solid slab first, which requires cooking it stiff enough to hold and then chilling it properly. Once it is cold it cuts cleanly into batons that hold their shape in a hot oven.

That structure is also what gives you the texture worth making these for: a crisp, almost crackly exterior with a soft, custardy interior. Frying them gets you there faster, but the oven version is close enough and much less trouble.

## Coarse, not fine

This is the opposite of what a polenta cake wants.

Fine polenta makes a smooth, dense slab that turns slightly gluey when baked. Coarse polenta keeps distinct grains, which is what produces the rough surface that crisps and the interior that stays light. If the bag says "instant" or "quick-cook", it will work but the result will be softer.

## Stock again

Same warning as every other savoury recipe on this site, and it applies with unusual force here.

Polenta absorbs its entire cooking liquid. Nothing is drained off, nothing is discarded. Whatever is in the stock ends up concentrated in the finished chips at full strength.

A standard chicken or vegetable stock cube is largely salt, onion and garlic. Use a certified low-FODMAP stock, homemade stock, or plain salted water with an extra tablespoon of parmesan to make up the savoury difference. Water works better than people assume — the cheese, butter and rosemary are carrying most of the flavour regardless.
