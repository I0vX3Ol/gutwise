Cooking at home on this diet is a solved problem within a fortnight. You learn the swaps, you restock the cupboard, and it stops requiring thought.

Restaurants are the part that stays difficult, and they are where most people first consider giving up. Someone else is cooking, you cannot see the ingredients, and the two things you most need to avoid are in almost every commercial kitchen's base preparation.

## Why restaurants are genuinely harder

It is not fussiness or bad luck. It is structural.

Professional kitchens build flavour from a small number of prepared bases made in bulk — stock, mirepoix or soffritto, mother sauces, marinades, dressings, spice rubs. Nearly all of them start with onion and garlic. That base then goes into dozens of dishes on the menu.

So a grilled chicken breast, which sounds like the safest possible order, may have been brined in a seasoned solution, rested in a garlic marinade and finished with a pan sauce built on stock. Nothing on the menu description mentions any of it, and the server has no reason to think it is relevant.

This is why "I'll just have something plain" fails so often. Plain, in a restaurant, is rarely plain.

## Cuisines that tend to work

Some kitchens are structurally easier than others.

**Japanese** is the strongest option overall. Sashimi, plain rice, grilled fish, edamame in small portions and simple noodle dishes are common, and the flavour base is more often soy, dashi, ginger and mirin than allium. Ask about the dashi and the dipping sauces.

**Grill houses and steakhouses** work because the cooking method is exactly what you want: a piece of meat over heat. Ask for it plain with oil and salt, no marinade, no sauce, and take a baked potato or plain rice alongside.

**Indian**, counterintuitively, can work with careful ordering. Plain rice, plain grilled tandoori meats and plain dosa are often fine. The curries are not — onion and garlic are the foundation of nearly every sauce.

**Thai and Vietnamese** offer plain rice, grilled meats, rice noodle dishes and fresh rolls. Fish sauce is fine. Watch for garlic in almost everything else, and be careful of anything described as a stir-fry sauce.

Harder: Italian, where the tomato sauces are almost always soffritto-based; Mexican, where beans, onion and garlic run through most dishes; anywhere heavily reliant on gravies, chowders or dressings.

## What to actually say

The words you choose change the response more than most people expect.

Do **not** lead with "I'm on a low-FODMAP diet." Almost no server has heard of it, and it sounds like a diet in the weight-loss sense — a preference to be accommodated if convenient.

Do say: **"I have an intolerance to onion and garlic. Eating them makes me quite unwell. Can you tell me which dishes are made without them?"**

That is specific, it names a consequence, and it asks a question the kitchen can actually answer. It also names the two ingredients that account for the overwhelming majority of the risk, rather than handing over a list of twenty things.

Do not claim an allergy you do not have. It is untrue, it triggers a much more disruptive kitchen protocol involving cleaned surfaces and separate equipment, and it erodes the seriousness with which those protocols get treated for people whose lives depend on them.

### Three questions that do most of the work

- **"Is the stock onion-free?"** — the most common hidden source, in soups, risottos, sauces and braises.
- **"Was this marinated or brined?"** — the reason a plain-sounding protein is not plain.
- **"Can I have it without the sauce, or the sauce on the side?"** — often the single change that makes a dish workable.

## Practical tactics

**Look at the menu before you go.** Deciding in advance removes the pressure of choosing while a table waits, which is when people default to whatever sounds safest and get it wrong.

**Call at an off hour.** Three in the afternoon gets you a considered answer from someone with time. Eight on a Friday gets you a guess.

**Order simply and add.** A plainly grilled protein plus a plain starch plus a green salad with oil and vinegar is dull, and it works nearly everywhere. Bring the interest yourself if you want — a small bottle of garlic-infused oil in a bag is not ridiculous during elimination.

**Eat something before you go.** Not to avoid eating, but so that hunger is not making the decision if the options turn out to be poor.

## At other people's houses

This is more socially delicate than a restaurant and usually easier in practice, because you can talk to the cook directly and well in advance.

Tell them early rather than at the table — a week ahead, not on arrival. Give them the short version: no onion, no garlic, no wheat, and lactose-free dairy or none. Offer to bring a dish, which solves the problem and reads as generous rather than difficult. And accept that a host who has cooked for you will sometimes get it wrong anyway.

## Travel

The dependable pattern is to control breakfast and one other meal, and be flexible about the rest.

Supermarkets are more useful than restaurants. Rice cakes, hard cheese, plain nuts, bananas, oranges, tinned fish and lactose-free milk are available almost everywhere and require no negotiation. A hotel room with a kettle covers plain oats.

For long flights, order a gluten-free meal in advance. It is not a low-FODMAP meal and will not be safe by default, but it removes wheat from the tray and is more likely to be a plainly cooked protein and rice than the standard option.

## When it goes wrong

It will, and the correct response is smaller than it feels.

Symptoms from a single exposure usually settle within a day or two. One bad meal does not undo the elimination phase, does not reset any clock, and does not invalidate the weeks that came before it. There is nothing to repair.

Write down what you ate and what happened. During reintroduction that record turns out to be genuinely useful — accidental exposures often produce the first real evidence about which group is actually a problem for you.

And if you are reacting to most meals out despite ordering carefully, the culprit is almost always stock or a sauce. Neither is visible on the plate, and neither will appear in the menu description.
